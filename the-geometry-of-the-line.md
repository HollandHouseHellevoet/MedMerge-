# The Geometry of the Line

---

You write insurance checks. They clear. You do this twelve times a year. At renewal, a broker shows you a number that is larger than last year's number, you negotiate it down to a number that is still larger than last year's number, and you sign. The check clears again.

This is not new information. You have been doing this for your entire career. Every CFO has. The total cost of employer-sponsored health coverage in the United States exceeds $1.3 trillion annually. Your share, for a company of 2,500 employees, is somewhere between $35 million and $42 million per year. You know this. You budget for it. You consider it a fixed cost of operations, in the same category as rent or electricity: necessary, unpleasant, not worth rethinking.

That is where most people stop.

Here is a question nobody has asked you: where does the money go after the check clears?

Not in theory. Specifically. Follow one dollar from the moment it leaves your operating account.

Roughly 83 cents pays claims. Your employees got sick, visited doctors, filled prescriptions. The care was delivered. That portion of the dollar did what you thought the entire dollar was doing. Set it aside.

The remaining 17 cents is more interesting.

A portion covers the carrier's operating costs. Administration. Compliance. Network management. Call it 8 to 10 cents. Another portion pays your broker. Two to 4 cents. What remains, 3 to 7 cents on each dollar, goes somewhere that never appears on any invoice you have ever received.

It becomes surplus.

Surplus is the carrier's retained capital. It sits on their balance sheet as equity. UnitedHealth Group holds $25.5 billion of it. Elevance holds $40.3 billion. These are not fees. They are not costs. They are capital accumulation. Your premium dollar funded it. You own none of it.

But surplus is only half the story. The more important half is timing.

Your premium arrives at the carrier on the first of the month. The claims against that premium are paid over the following 30, 60, 90 days. Some claims take longer. During that gap, the carrier holds your money. This is called float.

Float is not a technical term that matters only to actuaries. Float is the most powerful capital mechanism in modern finance. It is other people's money, held temporarily, invested permanently.

One person understood this better than anyone.

Warren Buffett did not build Berkshire Hathaway by picking stocks. He built it by acquiring insurance companies. Not because insurance is a good business. Because insurance generates float. Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries held $168 billion in float by the end of 2023. Buffett has called it, repeatedly, the single most important factor in Berkshire's growth. He collects premiums today. He pays claims later. In between, he invests. The returns on that invested float built one of the most valuable institutions in human history.

He has been doing this for sixty years. He has been explicit about the mechanics. There is no secret.

The carriers processing your employees' claims operate the identical model. They collect your premium on day one. They invest it immediately. They pay claims on day 45, day 60, day 90. The investment returns generated during that window belong to the carrier. Not to you. For a company paying $42 million in annual premium, the float your dollars generate produces approximately $230,000 to $310,000 per year in investment income at current rates. For the carrier. Every year. From your capital.

Add the underwriting margin: 3 to 5 percent of premium that the carrier retains as profit after claims and expenses. On your $42 million, that is $1.26 million to $2.1 million. Per year.

Add the surplus accumulation: the equity that builds on the carrier's balance sheet from retained earnings on your premium stream. Compounding annually.

Over a decade, a single 2,500-employee company contributes $56 million to $75 million in surplus, float income, and underwriting profit to its carrier. Capital that left your balance sheet, generated returns, and built someone else's institution.

You funded it. You own none of it. You never will.

Sit with that for a moment.

---

The natural next question is: what would it take to sit at the other end of that line?

To be the entity that holds the float. That retains the underwriting margin. That accumulates the surplus. To stop being the point of origin and start being the destination.

Think about it before reading further. The obvious answers come quickly. They are all wrong.

Become a carrier. File for licensure. Post statutory reserves. Build a claims operation, a compliance department, a provider network. Hire actuaries. Submit to state regulatory examination. This requires $50 million to $100 million in startup capital, three to five years of regulatory process, and expertise your company does not have and should not build. You are not in the insurance business. You should not be.

Form a cooperative. Pool purchasing power with other employers. Negotiate better rates. This is what purchasing coalitions have done for thirty years. It does not change the underlying economics. You are still writing a check to a carrier. The float still belongs to them. The surplus still builds on their balance sheet. You negotiated a discount on someone else's product. The line did not move.

Hire a better broker. Switch carriers. Self-insure with a traditional TPA. These are variations on the same geometry. The players change. The structure does not. Your capital still flows in one direction. No equity returns.

Every obvious answer fails for the same reason: it tries to improve the employer's position within the existing architecture. The architecture is the problem.

---

There is a company called Fiserv that is worth understanding.

Fiserv does not own a single bank. It owns no deposits. It holds no banking charter. It makes no loans. And yet, more than one in three financial institutions in the United States cannot process a transaction without Fiserv's infrastructure. Core banking systems. Payment rails. Digital platforms. Compliance engines. The technology stack that makes a bank function.

Each bank on the Fiserv network is independent. Each bank has its own customers, its own brand, its own balance sheet. No bank is locked in by contract in any meaningful sense; migration is always theoretically possible. But migration means rebuilding core operations from scratch. Rewriting integrations. Retraining staff. Recreating what Fiserv provides at scale for a fraction of what it would cost alone.

The banks stay. The economics flow to Fiserv. Not because Fiserv owns the banks. Because Fiserv owns what the banks cannot operate without.

This distinction is worth pausing on. Ownership and control are not the same thing. They have never been the same thing. Rockefeller did not need to own every oil well. He owned the refineries and the railroads. Carnegie did not need to own every construction project. He owned the steel mills and the shipping routes. The most durable economic positions in history were not built by owning the end product. They were built by owning the infrastructure the end product depends on.

Now apply this to the question you were already asking.

You do not need to become a carrier. You do not need to own an insurance company. You need to own the infrastructure that sits between the employer and the claim. The rails.

---

Picture a company with 2,500 employees. Call it the anchor. Not because it manages anything. Because its scale makes a structure viable.

The anchor brings its existing premium, $42 million annually, inside a collective structure. Not a carrier. Not a cooperative. An entity that owns the operational infrastructure: claims administration, pharmacy management, stop-loss placement, compliance, analytics. The rails.

Thirty or forty other employers in the region do the same. Collectively: 50,000 to 100,000 covered lives. $800 million to $1.6 billion in annual premium flowing through infrastructure the collective owns.

Now watch what happens to the money.

The 83 cents that pays claims still pays claims. Nothing changes there. Care is delivered. Providers are paid. The employee experience is identical.

The 17 cents changes entirely.

Administrative costs drop. Shared infrastructure operating at scale costs less per life than fifty separate bilateral vendor relationships. The anchor's administrative burden falls 35 to 45 percent. Savings: $630,000 to $1.08 million per year.

The float stays inside the collective. The same 45 to 60 day gap between premium collection and claims payment still exists. But the investment returns, $230,000 to $310,000 annually on the anchor's premium base alone, accrue to the collective. Not to a carrier. To the structure the employers own.

The underwriting margin, $1.26 million to $2.1 million per year on the anchor's volume, stays inside the collective as surplus. Accumulating. Compounding. Building equity on a balance sheet the employers hold.

The 50 vendor relationships the anchor managed individually consolidate to 12 to 15, negotiated on behalf of 50,000 to 100,000 lives instead of 2,500. Pharmacy costs decline 8 to 14 percent on formulary optimization. Stop-loss premiums decline 15 to 25 percent on actuarial credibility. The 18,000 individual contracts, invoices, and service agreements collapse to approximately 4,000.

Over five years, the anchor's accumulated equity interest in the collective, conservatively: $8 million to $12 million. Capital that did not exist on the balance sheet before. Created by redirecting money that was already being spent.

But the structure matters more than the numbers.

Membership is immediate. Any employer that meets underwriting criteria joins and accesses the shared infrastructure from day one. Ownership is different. Ownership vests over five years. Each year of participation accrues an equity stake in the collective's surplus, its reserves, its infrastructure, its contracts.

No employer is locked in. Any employer can leave at any time. But an employer that leaves walks away from accumulated equity in a growing institution. The same calculation a partner makes before leaving a law firm. The same calculation a policyholder makes before leaving a mutual insurer. The incentive to stay is not contractual. It is economic. It is the most durable kind.

Northwestern Mutual, MassMutual, Guardian: these institutions survived for more than a century because the people who funded them were the same people who owned them. The incentives were aligned. The structure was self-reinforcing.

Each rail added to the collective's infrastructure deepens this. Payroll processing. HR administration. Tax filing. Accounting. Every function that moves onto the shared rails reduces the employer's standalone cost and increases the collective's density. The anchor does not acquire its vendors. It becomes the infrastructure they cannot operate without.

The Fiserv position. Applied to employer capital. Owned by the employers themselves.

The line did not just move. It reversed.

---

The premium dollar is leaving your balance sheet this month. It left last month. It will leave next month. Somewhere, it is building an institution. The only thing that has ever been in question is whether that institution is yours.
