# The Geometry of the Line

A teaching document on capital structure, institutional ownership, and the employer balance sheet.

---

## I. The Capital Problem You Already Have

The average American employer with 2,500 employees spends between $35 million and $42 million annually on health insurance premiums. Over a decade, that is $350 million to $420 million in capital that leaves the balance sheet permanently.

This is not a benefits problem. It is a capital allocation problem.

Every CFO in the country can recite their cost of capital to two decimal places. Very few can answer a simpler question: where does the $16,000 per employee per year go after it leaves payroll?

The answer is instructive. Roughly 82 to 85 cents of every premium dollar pays claims. The remainder, 15 to 18 cents, funds carrier administration, profit margins, and reserves. For a 2,500-life employer, that remainder is $5.6 million to $7.5 million per year. Over ten years: $56 million to $75 million.

That capital does not disappear. It accumulates. It compounds. It builds surplus on the carrier's balance sheet, funds their real estate portfolio, pays their executive compensation, and generates investment returns that belong to their shareholders.

The employer funded the institution. The employer owns none of it.

This is not a complaint. It is a structural observation. The premium dollar is not a cost. It is an involuntary capital contribution to someone else's enterprise. Every employer in America is a silent limited partner in an institution they did not choose, cannot influence, and will never share in.

The question is not whether this is expensive. The question is whether it is necessary.

## II. The Geometry of the Line

Executives model their insurance spend as a line segment. It starts at payroll. It ends at the carrier. Twelve payments per year, a renewal negotiation, a broker in the middle. Simple.

The line is not a segment. It is infinite. And the carrier is not at the end of it.

Follow the premium dollar past the carrier's front door. The claims portion, roughly 83 cents, moves through a network of intermediaries: pharmacy benefit managers, third-party administrators, specialty networks, each extracting margin before the dollar reaches the provider who delivered the care. The administrative portion, roughly 17 cents, splits further: broker commissions, reinsurance premiums, carrier overhead, and surplus contributions.

The surplus is where the geometry gets interesting.

UnitedHealth Group reported $25.5 billion in shareholders' equity at year-end 2023. Elevance: $40.3 billion. These are not operating companies in the traditional sense. They are capital aggregation vehicles that happen to process health claims. Their investment portfolios generate billions in annual returns. Their underwriting profits compound year over year. Their float, the period between premium collection and claims payment, operates identically to the mechanism Warren Buffett has described as the engine of Berkshire Hathaway's capital structure.

Buffett has been explicit about this. Insurance float is capital held temporarily that behaves as if it were owned permanently, provided the cost of float is managed. Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries generated $168 billion in float by 2023. The carriers processing your employees' claims operate the same model. The float belongs to them. The investment returns belong to them. The underwriting profit belongs to them.

The employer's position in this geometry is fixed. You are the point of origin. The capital flows in one direction. No equity accrues to you. No float benefits you. No investment return reaches your balance sheet.

This is not an inefficiency. It is the architecture working exactly as designed.

The question is not who designed it. The question is whether the employer must remain at the origin, or whether the line can be redrawn.

## III. The Model from First Principles

Forget companies. Start with a concept.

A single employer of any size faces the insurance market alone. Their claims experience is their own. Their administrative costs are fixed. Their negotiating leverage is proportional to their headcount, which is to say: limited.

Now place 40 employers in a room. Collectively: 50,000 to 100,000 covered lives. $800 million to $1.6 billion in annual premium. Their combined claims volume makes them statistically credible. Their combined purchasing power makes them commercially significant. Their combined float is substantial.

The concept is not a purchasing group. Purchasing groups negotiate discounts on someone else's product. The concept is a collective that owns the infrastructure itself.

This requires an anchor.

An anchor is not a manager. An anchor is the largest employer in a regional collective, the entity whose scale and operational credibility makes the structure viable from day one. The anchor does not run the collective. The anchor makes the collective real. A 2,500-life employer anchoring a regional collective of 40 companies provides the actuarial base, the administrative volume, and the institutional gravity that smaller employers cannot generate alone.

Around the anchor, a vendor ecosystem forms. Not a single vendor. A curated network: third-party administrators, pharmacy benefit managers, stop-loss carriers, actuaries, compliance counsel. Each vendor is selected by the collective. Each contract is held by the collective. The vendors serve the membership; they do not own the relationship.

This is the Fiserv model applied to employer capital.

Fiserv does not own the banks it serves. It provides the operational rails: core processing, payment networks, digital infrastructure. The banks retain their charters, their deposits, their customer relationships. Fiserv's value is the shared infrastructure that makes each bank more efficient than it could be alone. Remove a bank from the Fiserv network and the bank still functions, but at higher cost, with less capability, on inferior technology.

The collective operates identically. Each employer retains its own plan design, its own employee relationships, its own benefit structure. The collective provides the shared rails: claims administration, pharmacy management, compliance, analytics, stop-loss placement. Each rail added deepens the operational integration. Payroll processing. HR administration. Tax filing. Accounting services. Each function that moves onto the collective's infrastructure reduces the employer's standalone cost and increases the collective's density.

This is where ownership separates from membership.

Membership is immediate. Any employer that meets underwriting criteria joins the collective and accesses the shared infrastructure from day one. Ownership vests over five years. Each year of membership accrues an ownership stake in the collective's surplus, its reserves, its infrastructure, its vendor contracts. At full vesting, the employer is not a customer. The employer is a principal.

Five-year vesting creates permanence without contracts. No employer is locked in. Any employer can leave at any time. But an employer that leaves forfeits accumulated equity in a growing institution. The incentive to remain is not contractual. It is economic. It is the same incentive that keeps a partner at a law firm, an owner in a cooperative, a policyholder in a mutual.

The mutual analogy is precise. A mutual insurance company is owned by its policyholders. Surplus accrues to policyholders. Investment returns benefit policyholders. The mutual does not extract capital from its members; it accumulates capital on their behalf.

The American mutual insurance model built some of the most durable financial institutions in the country: Northwestern Mutual, MassMutual, Guardian. They survived because their incentive structure was aligned. The policyholders and the owners were the same people.

The collective applies this structure to employers. The premium dollar does not leave the balance sheet and vanish into a carrier's surplus. It enters a structure the employer progressively owns. The float is held for the collective's benefit. The underwriting margin stays inside the collective. The investment returns on reserves accrue to the members.

The Berkshire analogy completes the picture. Buffett did not invent insurance. He recognized that insurance float, managed at low cost, is the most efficient form of capital accumulation ever devised. He used float to acquire productive assets. The collective does not acquire external businesses. But the principle is identical: premium dollars, held in float, generating returns that compound for the benefit of the owners.

The difference: in Berkshire's model, Buffett's shareholders benefit. In the collective's model, the employers benefit. Because the employers are the shareholders.

Every rail added to the collective's infrastructure, every line of insurance consolidated, every administrative function integrated, deepens the employer's operational dependency on infrastructure it owns. This is not lock-in. This is ownership deepening. The employer is not trapped. The employer is building.

## IV. What This Means for an Anchor

Consider a specific company. 2,500 employees. $42 million in annual health insurance premium. 50 active vendor relationships across insurance, payroll, HR, accounting, and tax compliance. 18,000 individual contracts, invoices, and service agreements per year.

**The balance sheet before.**

The $42 million in annual premium is a line item: employee benefits expense. It generates no asset. It produces no return. It builds no equity. It appears on the income statement as a cost and on the cash flow statement as an outflow. That is the totality of its balance sheet effect. Over five years: $210 million in cumulative outflow, no residual asset.

The 50 vendor relationships are individually negotiated, individually managed, individually renewed. Administrative cost of vendor management across insurance, payroll, HR, accounting, and compliance: approximately $1.8 million to $2.4 million annually in fully-loaded personnel and overhead costs.

**The balance sheet after.**

The same $42 million in annual premium enters the collective. Claims are paid from the collective's pool. Approximately $34.9 million (83 cents on the dollar) pays claims, the same as before. The remaining $7.1 million does not leave the employer's economic interest.

Administrative costs drop. The collective's shared infrastructure handles claims processing, compliance, vendor management, and analytics at scale. The employer's administrative burden reduces by an estimated 35 to 45 percent. Savings: $630,000 to $1.08 million annually.

The float works for the collective. Average float duration on a $42 million premium base: 45 to 60 days. At a conservative 4.5 percent annualized return, float income attributable to a 2,500-life anchor: approximately $230,000 to $310,000 per year. This is income that previously accrued to the carrier.

Underwriting margin, historically 3 to 5 percent of premium, stays inside the collective. For a $42 million premium base: $1.26 million to $2.1 million per year. Previously: carrier profit. Now: collective surplus, accruing to the employer's ownership stake.

Over five years, at full vesting, the anchor employer holds a proportional equity stake in a collective managing $800 million to $1.2 billion in aggregate premium. The employer's accumulated surplus interest, conservatively: $8 million to $12 million. This is equity that did not exist on the employer's balance sheet before. It was created by redirecting capital that was already being spent.

**The vendor relationships before and after.**

Before: 50 vendors, 18,000 contracts, bilateral negotiations, fragmented data, no leverage beyond the employer's own volume.

After: the collective negotiates on behalf of 50,000 to 100,000 lives. Pharmacy costs decline 8 to 14 percent on formulary optimization alone. Stop-loss premiums decline 15 to 25 percent on improved actuarial credibility. Network discounts deepen 5 to 10 percent on aggregate volume. The employer's 50 vendor relationships consolidate to 12 to 15, managed through the collective's shared infrastructure. The 18,000 contracts reduce to approximately 4,000. Administrative complexity drops by more than half.

**The industry position before and after.**

Before: one employer, negotiating with carriers and vendors from a position of structural disadvantage, generating no institutional capital, building no lasting asset.

After: an anchor in a regional collective, holding an ownership stake in shared infrastructure, generating surplus and float income, operating at lower cost with deeper vendor leverage, and sitting at the center of an ecosystem that grows more valuable with every employer that joins.

The anchor did not spend more money. The anchor redirected money it was already spending into a structure it progressively owns. The capital that was building someone else's institution is now building the anchor's own.

## V.

Every year, the premium dollar leaves. Every year, it builds an institution. The only question an executive needs to answer is whose.
